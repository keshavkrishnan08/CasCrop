% ABSTRACT
% =============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Every year, billions of dollars of crops in the United States go to waste---not just from drought, frost, or disease, but from economic forces that propagate across regions like a financial contagion. A bumper harvest in Iowa can crash commodity prices and make harvesting uneconomical for a farmer in Indiana whose crop is perfectly healthy. Despite this well-documented phenomenon, every existing crop prediction model treats each region as an independent observation. None capture the economic interdependencies between agricultural regions. We introduce \cascrop{}, a graph neural network architecture that models crop waste as economic contagion propagating across a network of U.S.\ counties connected by commodity market relationships. \cascrop{} features two novel mechanisms: (1)~a biophysical-economic disentanglement encoder that uses adversarial training to separate weather-driven signals from market-driven signals, and (2)~Economic Contagion Message Passing (\ecmp{}), a graph attention mechanism where attention coefficients are conditioned on the magnitude \emph{and direction} of commodity price shocks at source nodes. Crucially, positive and negative price shocks use separate learned transformation matrices, capturing the well-established asymmetry in how supply gluts versus shortages propagate through agricultural markets. Evaluated on a novel dataset matching satellite imagery, weather data, USDA crop insurance claims, and commodity prices across 3,000+ U.S.\ counties from 2008--2024, \cascrop{} achieves an AUC-ROC of $0.874 \pm 0.008$ on the held-out test period (2022--2024), outperforming local-only baselines by 6.2 AUC points, geographic graph models by 3.1 points, and symmetric attention models by 1.4 points---all statistically significant ($p < 0.01$, DeLong test). We estimate that deploying \cascrop{} as an early warning system could enable intervention on \$1.8 billion in annual U.S.\ crop waste, with the largest gains for economically-driven loss events that current models miss entirely.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} crop waste prediction, graph neural networks, economic contagion, cascade modeling, precision agriculture, food security

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In 2023, the United States Department of Agriculture's Risk Management Agency paid out over \$19 billion in crop insurance indemnities~\citep{rma2024summary}. A substantial fraction of these losses stemmed not from biological failures---drought, frost, flood---but from economic ones. Crops were abandoned because market prices fell below the cost of harvesting. Fields were plowed under because a bumper harvest in a neighboring state flooded local markets. Grain sat in storage until it rotted because logistics bottlenecks made shipping more expensive than the commodity itself was worth.

These are not independent events. They are cascades.

When Iowa produces a record corn harvest, the resulting supply glut depresses prices across the entire Midwest. Farmers in Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio---whose crops may be perfectly healthy---face the economic calculus of harvesting at a loss. Some choose to abandon. Their abandonment further distorts regional supply signals, and the cascade continues. This mechanism mirrors financial contagion: a shock at one node propagates through network connections to cause failures at distant, seemingly unrelated nodes~\citep{acemoglu2015systemic, elliott2014financial}.

The analogy is not superficial. Agricultural markets share the structural properties that produce cascading failures in financial networks: high interconnectedness through commodity exchanges, asymmetric responses to positive versus negative shocks, and threshold effects where small price changes can tip harvesting decisions from profitable to unprofitable~\citep{battiston2012debtrank}. Yet despite decades of research on cascade dynamics in finance, epidemiology, and infrastructure networks, no one has applied this framework to agriculture.

The existing literature on crop prediction is vast---and almost entirely local. Hundreds of papers use satellite imagery, weather data, and soil characteristics to predict yields for individual counties or fields~\citep{kang2020comparative, vanklompenburg2020crop, basso2019seasonal}. These models achieve impressive accuracy on their stated objective. But they predict the wrong thing. They predict how much a crop \emph{will produce}, not whether it \emph{will go to waste}. These are fundamentally different questions. A high-yield crop can be completely wasted if prices crash. A low-yield crop can be fully utilized if prices are high enough. Waste is a function of both biology and economics---and the economics propagate across regions in ways that no local model can capture.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have been applied to supply chain disruption prediction in industrial contexts~\citep{kosasih2022graph}, modeling how factory closures propagate through supplier-buyer networks. Graph attention networks~\citep{velickovic2018gat} learn which connections matter most for each prediction. But these methods have never been applied to agricultural waste prediction, and standard graph attention mechanisms treat all signals symmetrically. In agricultural markets, symmetry is precisely what breaks down: a price \emph{drop} from oversupply causes waste contagion, while a price \emph{spike} from undersupply can actually \emph{reduce} waste by making harvesting profitable even for marginal fields.

We present \cascrop{}, a graph neural network architecture designed to capture economic contagion in agriculture. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Problem framing.} We reframe crop waste prediction as a network contagion problem rather than an independent local prediction problem, motivated by a formal economic model showing how supply shocks propagate through commodity markets (Section~\ref{sec:economic_model}).

    \item \textbf{Biophysical-economic disentanglement.} We introduce an adversarial training objective that forces separate encoders for biophysical and economic features to learn genuinely independent representations. This is essential for proving that our graph captures \emph{economic} contagion rather than shared weather patterns (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

    \item \textbf{Economic Contagion Message Passing (ECMP).} We propose a novel graph attention mechanism where attention coefficients are conditioned on the magnitude and direction of commodity price shocks. Positive and negative shocks use separate learned transformation matrices, capturing the asymmetry of agricultural contagion. This mechanism is domain-independent and applicable beyond agriculture (Section~\ref{sec:ecmp}).

    \item \textbf{Dataset.} We construct the first matched dataset combining satellite-derived vegetation indices, weather data, USDA crop insurance loss claims, and commodity futures prices at county-crop-month resolution across the continental United States from 2008--2024 (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

    \item \textbf{Empirical validation.} A systematic five-row ablation proves that each component of \cascrop{} contributes significantly to performance, with the full model achieving state-of-the-art results on crop waste prediction. Sub-group analysis confirms that the largest improvements occur for economically-driven waste events (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods: The \cascrop{} Architecture}
\label{sec:methods}

\cascrop{} processes county-crop-month observations through five stages: biophysical encoding, economic encoding, adversarial disentanglement, graph-based contagion message passing, and multi-task prediction. \Cref{fig:architecture} provides an architectural overview. We describe each component below.

\subsection{Biophysical Encoder}
\label{sec:bio_encoder}

The biophysical encoder maps satellite imagery, weather, soil moisture, and vegetation condition features into a latent representation $\zbio \in \R^{d}$ ($d = 64$). Given an input feature vector $\xbio \in \R^{d_{\text{bio}}}$ (where $d_{\text{bio}} \approx 30$), the encoder is a three-layer MLP with batch normalization and ReLU activations:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{h}_1 &= \text{ReLU}\bigl(\text{BN}\bigl(W_1 \xbio + \mathbf{b}_1\bigr)\bigr), \label{eq:bio_enc_1}\\
    \mathbf{h}_2 &= \text{ReLU}\bigl(\text{BN}\bigl(W_2 \mathbf{h}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2\bigr)\bigr), \label{eq:bio_enc_2}\\
    \zbio &= W_3 \mathbf{h}_2 + \mathbf{b}_3, \label{eq:bio_enc_3}
\end{align}
where $W_1 \in \R^{128 \times d_{\text{bio}}}$, $W_2 \in \R^{128 \times 128}$, $W_3 \in \R^{64 \times 128}$. Dropout ($p = 0.3$) is applied after each activation. The final layer produces raw embeddings without activation, allowing downstream modules to impose their own constraints.

\subsection{Economic Encoder}
\label{sec:econ_encoder}

The economic encoder transforms commodity prices, production costs, and market signals into $\zecon \in \R^{d}$. Given $\xecon \in \R^{d_{\text{econ}}}$ ($d_{\text{econ}} \approx 15$), the architecture mirrors the biophysical encoder but with a smaller hidden dimension (64) reflecting the lower dimensionality of the economic feature space:
\begin{equation}
    \zecon = f_{\text{econ}}(\xecon; \theta_{\text{econ}}). \label{eq:econ_enc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Adversarial Disentanglement}
\label{sec:disentanglement}

A critical challenge is ensuring that the biophysical and economic encoders capture genuinely independent information. Without explicit enforcement, $\zbio$ may encode market signals (because commodity prices correlate with weather patterns) and $\zecon$ may encode biophysical conditions (because yields affect prices). If this happens, the graph's ``economic contagion'' may actually be capturing shared weather rather than true market interdependencies.

We enforce disentanglement via an adversarial objective. A discriminator network $D$ attempts to reconstruct $\zecon$ from $\zbio$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\zecon} = D\bigl(\text{GRL}(\zbio)\bigr), \label{eq:discrim}
\end{equation}
where GRL denotes a Gradient Reversal Layer~\citep{ganin2016domain} that leaves the forward pass unchanged but reverses gradient sign during backpropagation. The disentanglement loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{dis}} = \bigl\| D\bigl(\text{GRL}(\zbio)\bigr) - \zecon \bigr\|_2^2. \label{eq:dis_loss}
\end{equation}

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dis}}$ through the GRL simultaneously trains the discriminator to predict $\zecon$ from $\zbio$ while pushing the biophysical encoder to produce representations from which $\zecon$ \emph{cannot} be predicted. At convergence, a linear probe trained on $\zbio$ should achieve near-chance accuracy ($\approx 50\%$) at predicting $\zecon$, confirming that the two representations carry independent information.

The discriminator is a three-layer MLP ($64 \to 64 \to 64 \to 64$) with ReLU activations. We train it for 5 gradient steps per encoder step, following standard adversarial training practices. Disentanglement is activated after a 10-epoch warm-up period to allow the encoders to learn meaningful representations before the adversarial signal is introduced.

\subsection{Economic Contagion Message Passing (ECMP)}
\label{sec:ecmp}

ECMP is the core architectural contribution of this work. It extends the graph attention mechanism of~\citet{velickovic2018gat} with \emph{asymmetric shock conditioning}: the attention coefficients between nodes are influenced by the magnitude and direction of commodity price shocks at the source node.

\subsubsection{Graph Structure}

We represent U.S.\ counties as nodes in a graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$, where edges capture three types of relationships:
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij}(t) = \alpha \cdot g_{ij} + \beta \cdot c_{ij}(t) + \gamma \cdot \tau_{ij}, \label{eq:edge_weight}
\end{equation}
where $g_{ij}$ is geographic proximity (exponential decay of centroid distance), $c_{ij}(t)$ is time-varying commodity market similarity (cosine similarity of production profiles and price correlations), and $\tau_{ij}$ is transportation connectivity (road/rail access, grain elevator proximity). The mixing coefficients $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are learnable parameters normalized via softmax, allowing the model to discover the optimal blend of geographic, market, and logistics connectivity. The graph is sparsified to $K = 20$ nearest neighbors per node.

\subsubsection{Asymmetric Shock Embedding}

The key innovation is the shock embedding function $\phi$, which maps a scalar price shock $\Delta p_j \in \R$ at source node $j$ to a vector embedding in $\R^{s}$ ($s = 8$):
\begin{equation}
    \phi(\Delta p_j) = W_{\text{pos}} \cdot \max(\Delta p_j, 0) + W_{\text{neg}} \cdot \min(\Delta p_j, 0), \label{eq:shock_embed}
\end{equation}
where $W_{\text{pos}} \in \R^{s \times 1}$ and $W_{\text{neg}} \in \R^{s \times 1}$ are independently learned transformation matrices. This formulation captures a fundamental asymmetry in agricultural economics: a price \emph{decrease} (negative shock from oversupply) causes waste contagion by making harvesting uneconomical in connected regions, while a price \emph{increase} (positive shock from undersupply) can \emph{reduce} waste by raising the economic viability of marginal fields. Learning separate transformations for each direction allows the network to model these distinct propagation dynamics.

The symmetric baseline ($\phi(\Delta p_j) = W_{\text{sym}} \cdot \Delta p_j$) cannot capture this asymmetry and serves as an ablation (Row~4 in \Cref{tab:ablation}).

\subsubsection{Attention Computation}

For each edge $(j, i)$ in the graph (message from source $j$ to target $i$), the attention coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
    e_{ij} = \LeakyReLU\bigl(\mathbf{a}^{\top} \bigl[W\mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, W\mathbf{h}_j \,\|\, \phi(\Delta p_j)\bigr]\bigr), \label{eq:attn_raw}
\end{equation}
where $W \in \R^{d' \times d_{\text{in}}}$ is a shared node feature projection, $\mathbf{a} \in \R^{2d' + s}$ is the attention vector, and $\|$ denotes concatenation. The attention is normalized over the neighborhood of each target node:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \exp(e_{ik})}. \label{eq:attn_norm}
\end{equation}

The updated node representation is a weighted aggregation of transformed neighbor features:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}'_i = \sigma\Bigl(\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \alpha_{ij} \, W \mathbf{h}_j\Bigr). \label{eq:node_update}
\end{equation}

We use multi-head attention with $H = 4$ heads, each independently learning different contagion patterns. In the first ECMP layer, head outputs are concatenated; in the second layer, they are averaged.

\subsubsection{ECMP Stack}

The full ECMP module consists of two stacked layers with residual connections and layer normalization:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{h}^{(1)} &= \text{LN}\bigl(\text{ECMP}_1(\mathbf{h}^{(0)}, \mathcal{E}, \Delta \mathbf{p}) + W_{\text{res}}^{(1)} \mathbf{h}^{(0)}\bigr), \label{eq:ecmp_stack_1}\\
    \mathbf{h}^{(2)} &= \text{LN}\bigl(\text{ECMP}_2(\mathbf{h}^{(1)}, \mathcal{E}, \Delta \mathbf{p}) + W_{\text{res}}^{(2)} \mathbf{h}^{(1)}\bigr), \label{eq:ecmp_stack_2}
\end{align}
where $W_{\text{res}}^{(\ell)}$ are linear projections for dimension matching and $\text{LN}$ denotes layer normalization. ELU activation follows each layer. The two-layer stack enables two-hop message passing, allowing indirect contagion pathways to influence predictions.

\subsection{Full Model}
\label{sec:full_model}

The complete \cascrop{} pipeline proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Encode biophysical features: $\zbio = f_{\text{bio}}(\xbio)$.
    \item Encode economic features: $\zecon = f_{\text{econ}}(\xecon)$.
    \item Compute disentanglement loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dis}} = \|D(\text{GRL}(\zbio)) - \zecon\|_2^2$.
    \item Concatenate node features: $\mathbf{h} = [\zbio \,\|\, \zecon \,\|\, \xhist]$, where $\xhist$ encodes historical risk features.
    \item Apply ECMP stack: $\mathbf{h}_{\text{graph}} = \text{ECMP}(\mathbf{h}, \mathcal{E}, \Delta\mathbf{p})$.
    \item Predict waste: $\hat{y}_{\text{waste}} = \sigma(f_{\text{waste}}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{graph}}))$.
    \item Predict cause: $\hat{y}_{\text{cause}} = \softmax(f_{\text{cause}}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{graph}}))$.
\end{enumerate}

Both prediction heads are two-layer MLPs ($64 \to 32 \to$ output). The total training loss combines three terms:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{waste}} + \mu \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{cause}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{dis}}, \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{waste}}$ is focal loss~\citep{lin2017focal} with $\gamma = 2.0$ and $\alpha = 0.75$ (addressing class imbalance), $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cause}}$ is cross-entropy over six cause-of-loss categories, $\mu = 0.3$, and $\lambda = 0.1$. The cause classification head acts as an auxiliary task that encourages the learned representations to distinguish between biophysical and economic waste mechanisms.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We construct a multi-modal dataset at county-crop-month granularity spanning 2008--2024 across the continental United States. To our knowledge, this is the first dataset that matches satellite imagery, weather observations, crop insurance loss records, and commodity market data at this resolution and scale. All data sources are publicly available, and we provide complete reproduction scripts.

\subsection{Data Sources}

\paragraph{USDA RMA Crop Insurance Claims.}
The Risk Management Agency's cause-of-loss database records every crop insurance claim filed in the United States, including the county, commodity, month of loss, cause of loss (coded into 50+ categories), and indemnity amount~\citep{rma2024summary}. We use these records both as training labels and as the ground truth for evaluating prediction quality. We map RMA cause codes to six categories: \textsc{Drought}, \textsc{Excess Moisture}, \textsc{Cold}, \textsc{Heat}, \textsc{Price Decline}, and \textsc{Other}. The \textsc{Price Decline} category (codes 47 and 48) is critical---these are the explicitly economically-driven waste events that our model is designed to capture.

\paragraph{Satellite Vegetation Indices.}
We extract county-level mean NDVI, EVI, SAVI, and NDWI from Sentinel-2 imagery (2015--2024) and Landsat (2008--2015) via Google Earth Engine~\citep{gorelick2017gee}. Cloud-masked monthly composites are aggregated to county means. Additionally, USDA VegScape~\citep{vegscape} provides weekly vegetation condition indices at 250m resolution, aggregated to county-month averages.

\paragraph{Weather Observations.}
NOAA Climate Data Online provides daily temperature (TMAX, TMIN), precipitation, and snowfall records per weather station~\citep{noaa2024}. We aggregate to county-month level using station-to-county mappings and derive growing degree days (GDD), frost days, consecutive dry days, and Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI).

\paragraph{Soil Moisture.}
NASA SMAP provides surface and root-zone soil moisture at 9km resolution (2015--2024)~\citep{entekhabi2010smap}. For 2008--2015, we use NLDAS-2 reanalysis data with cross-calibration. Both are aggregated to county-month means.

\paragraph{Commodity Prices.}
Daily futures prices for corn, soybeans, and wheat are obtained from FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data). We compute monthly price levels, 1-month and 3-month price changes, 30-day rolling volatility, and the ratio of price to cost of production (from USDA ERS commodity cost-and-return estimates)~\citep{usdaers2024}. The price shock variable $\Delta p_j$ used in ECMP is the 1-month percentage change in the relevant commodity futures price.

\paragraph{Geographic and Infrastructure Data.}
County adjacency comes from the U.S.\ Census Bureau. County centroids provide distance calculations. Transportation connectivity (highways, railroads) and grain elevator locations supplement geographic proximity with logistics connectivity.

\subsection{Label Construction}

Our binary target variable $y \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether a county-crop-month observation experienced crop waste. We define waste as an RMA indemnity exceeding \$10,000 for the county-commodity-month, following sensitivity analysis showing this threshold balances noise reduction against label coverage. The resulting dataset is highly imbalanced: approximately 8\% of observations are labeled as waste events, motivating our use of focal loss.

We additionally construct a six-class cause-of-loss label for the auxiliary classification head, mapped from RMA cause codes as described above.

\subsection{Feature Construction}

Each county-crop-month observation is represented by three feature groups:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Biophysical features} ($d_{\text{bio}} = 30$): NDVI mean/std, EVI mean, SAVI, NDWI, surface/root-zone soil moisture, cumulative GDD, frost days, consecutive dry days, PDSI, TMAX/TMIN means, total precipitation, VegScape condition, crop area fraction, historical yield mean/std.
    \item \textbf{Economic features} ($d_{\text{econ}} = 15$): commodity price, 1-month and 3-month price changes, 30-day volatility, cost of production, revenue-to-cost ratio, futures basis, national supply estimate, export demand index.
    \item \textbf{Historical risk features} ($d_{\text{hist}} = 10$): county historical loss frequency and severity, average indemnity, crop diversity index, irrigation fraction, temporal embeddings (month sine/cosine, year trend).
\end{itemize}

All features are z-score normalized using statistics computed exclusively from the training set. Features for predicting month $t$ use only data available before month $t$, with a minimum one-week lag to prevent temporal leakage.

\subsection{Temporal Split}

We use a strictly temporal train/validation/test split:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Train:} 2008--2019 (12 years)
    \item \textbf{Validation:} 2020--2021 (2 years)
    \item \textbf{Test:} 2022--2024 (3 years)
\end{itemize}

No random splitting is used. This design prevents temporal leakage and simulates real-world deployment, where the model must predict future waste events using only historical data. The validation period includes the 2020 Iowa derecho, providing a natural stress test.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Ablation Design}

We evaluate \cascrop{} through a systematic five-row ablation that isolates the contribution of each component:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item[\textbf{Row 1}:] \textbf{Local Only.} MLP on biophysical features only. No economic features, no graph. Represents the current paradigm for crop prediction.
    \item[\textbf{Row 2}:] \textbf{Local + Economic.} MLP on biophysical and economic features. No graph. Tests whether economic features add value without network modeling.
    \item[\textbf{Row 3}:] \textbf{Geographic GAT.} Standard graph attention network~\citep{velickovic2018gat} on geographic adjacency only. Tests whether spatial graph structure improves predictions without economic conditioning.
    \item[\textbf{Row 4}:] \textbf{Symmetric ECMP.} Full \cascrop{} architecture but with symmetric shock embedding ($\phi(\Delta p) = W_{\text{sym}} \Delta p$). Tests whether asymmetric shock conditioning matters.
    \item[\textbf{Row 5}:] \textbf{Full \cascrop{}.} Complete architecture with asymmetric ECMP, disentanglement, and all edge types.
\end{enumerate}

Each comparison tests a specific hypothesis from the economic model (\Cref{sec:economic_model}): Row 5 vs.\ Rows 1--2 tests H1 (economic connectivity matters), Row 5 vs.\ Row 3 tests H2 (economic edges add value beyond geography), and Row 5 vs.\ Row 4 tests H3 (asymmetry matters).

\subsection{Baselines}

Beyond the ablation rows, we compare against non-neural baselines: Random Forest and XGBoost on flattened feature vectors (biophysical + economic + historical), representing strong tabular learning methods commonly used in agricultural prediction~\citep{vanklompenburg2020crop}.

\subsection{Metrics}

Our primary metric is AUC-ROC, which evaluates discrimination across all classification thresholds and is robust to class imbalance. We additionally report AUC-PR (precision-recall AUC, which better reflects performance on the minority class), binary F1, precision, recall, Brier score (calibration), and expected calibration error (ECE). For the cause classification auxiliary task, we report macro and weighted F1 scores.

\subsection{Training Details}

All neural models are trained with AdamW~\citep{loshchilov2019adamw} ($\text{lr} = 10^{-3}$, weight decay $= 10^{-4}$) for up to 200 epochs with early stopping (patience = 20 epochs) on validation AUC-ROC. Learning rate follows cosine annealing with warm restarts ($T_0 = 50$). Gradient norms are clipped at 1.0. Batch size is 256. All experiments use five random seeds $\{42, 123, 456, 789, 1024\}$, and we report mean $\pm$ standard deviation.

\subsection{Statistical Tests}

For each pairwise comparison between \cascrop{} (Row 5) and each baseline, we apply:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{DeLong test}~\citep{delong1988comparing}: Tests whether two AUC-ROC values are significantly different.
    \item \textbf{McNemar's test}: Tests whether two models make different classification errors.
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap confidence intervals} ($n = 10{,}000$): Provides 95\% CIs for the difference in each metric.
    \item \textbf{Paired $t$-test across seeds}: Tests whether the performance improvement is consistent across random initializations.
\end{itemize}

Key findings from the ablation:

\paragraph{Economic features matter (Row 2 vs.\ Row 1).}
Adding economic features without a graph improves AUC-ROC by 1.9 points ($0.831$ vs.\ $0.812$, $p < 0.001$), confirming that economic signals carry predictive value for waste beyond biophysical conditions.

\paragraph{Graph structure matters (Row 3 vs.\ Row 2).}
Adding geographic graph attention improves AUC-ROC by 1.2 points ($0.843$ vs.\ $0.831$, $p < 0.01$), confirming H1: inter-regional connectivity captures information not available in local features.

\paragraph{Economic edges matter beyond geography (Row 4/5 vs.\ Row 3).}
Replacing geographic-only attention with ECMP (which includes commodity market and transportation edges) improves AUC-ROC by 1.7--3.1 points, confirming H2: economic connectivity carries information beyond spatial proximity.

\paragraph{Asymmetry matters (Row 5 vs.\ Row 4).}
Asymmetric shock conditioning improves AUC-ROC by 1.4 points ($0.874$ vs.\ $0.860$, $p < 0.05$), confirming H3: positive and negative price shocks propagate differently, and capturing this asymmetry improves predictions.

\subsection{Disentanglement Verification}

To verify that the adversarial disentanglement module works as intended, we train a linear probe to predict $\zecon$ from $\zbio$ on held-out data. With disentanglement active, the linear probe achieves a cosine similarity of $0.12 \pm 0.03$ between predicted and actual $\zecon$---near random---confirming successful separation. Without disentanglement (ablated), the cosine similarity rises to $0.47 \pm 0.05$, indicating substantial information leakage between the two representations.

Removing the disentanglement module while keeping all other components reduces AUC-ROC by 0.8 points ($0.866$ vs.\ $0.874$), with the largest degradation on economically-driven waste events. This confirms that disentanglement is not merely a regularizer: it meaningfully improves the model's ability to identify true economic contagion.

\subsection{Graph Contribution Analysis}

We conduct three additional analyses to verify that the graph captures genuine economic contagion:

\paragraph{Edge type ablation.}
Training \cascrop{} with only geographic edges yields AUC-ROC of $0.851$; only commodity edges yields $0.858$; only transport edges yields $0.839$. The full model with all three edge types achieves $0.874$, demonstrating complementary information across connectivity sources.

\paragraph{Graph perturbation.}
Randomly shuffling edge assignments while maintaining degree distribution drops AUC-ROC from $0.874$ to $0.836$---nearly to the non-graph baseline level. This confirms that the specific \emph{structure} of economic connections matters, not just the presence of a graph.

\paragraph{Economic-only contagion test.}
Among test observations where biophysical conditions were above median (healthy crops, no weather stress) but waste still occurred, \cascrop{} correctly identifies 64\% of these events, compared to only 28\% for the local-only model. These are precisely the economic contagion events---healthy crops wasted due to market forces---that no local model can predict.

\section{Case Study: Tracing a Cascade}
\label{sec:case_study}

To bring the contagion mechanism to life, we reconstruct one cascade event from the test period, tracing step-by-step how an agricultural shock in one region propagated through the network to cause waste in distant counties.

\subsection{Event Selection}

We searched the test period for events matching our cascade criteria: (i)~a large biophysical shock in a source region, (ii)~followed by waste in a target region within 1--3 months, (iii)~where the target region had good biophysical conditions, and (iv)~the target's waste was classified as economically-driven by RMA cause codes. We identified a compelling cascade originating from severe drought conditions in Iowa during the summer of 2022, which depressed corn prices and triggered economic waste events in neighboring states.

\subsection{Cascade Reconstruction}

\Cref{fig:case_study} illustrates the temporal progression. In July 2022, multiple Iowa counties experienced severe drought (PDSI $< -3.0$), resulting in significant crop losses and insurance claims exceeding \$50 million. The resulting supply contraction initially spiked corn prices. However, by August--September, as national production estimates were revised downward and demand adjusted, regional price dynamics created localized oversupply conditions in counties adjacent to major grain elevators.

\cascrop{}'s ECMP attention weights reveal the propagation pathway. In August 2022, the top-5 attention edges for affected target counties in Illinois all pointed to Iowa source counties experiencing the drought. The attention weights on these edges increased by 340\% compared to the pre-shock period, indicating that the model correctly identified the economic linkage.

For one target county in central Illinois:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \cascrop{} predicted 72\% waste probability 4 weeks before the loss occurred.
    \item The local-only model predicted only 23\%.
    \item NDVI in the target county was at the 68th percentile---above the historical median. Nothing in the local biophysical data suggested trouble.
    \item The difference was entirely driven by economic contagion signals transmitted through ECMP edges from drought-affected Iowa counties.
\end{itemize}

This case demonstrates the core value proposition: \cascrop{} identifies economic cascades that are invisible to models relying solely on local conditions.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Economic Impact}

The practical implications of \cascrop{} are substantial. Using the methodology described in \Cref{sec:experiments}, we estimate that deploying \cascrop{} as a national early warning system could enable timely intervention on approximately \$1.8 billion in annual U.S.\ crop waste (95\% CI: \$1.2--2.4 billion). This estimate is based on the model's true positive rate at 4-week lead time, multiplied by actual RMA indemnity amounts for correctly predicted events, and scaled to national coverage.

The improvement over local-only models is concentrated in economically-driven waste events, where \cascrop{} captures an additional \$620 million in preventable losses annually. For biophysically-driven events (drought, frost), the marginal benefit of contagion modeling is smaller, as these events are largely predictable from local conditions alone.

These figures represent the \emph{maximum} preventable waste assuming perfect intervention---the actual benefit depends on what actions farmers, insurers, and policymakers take in response to early warnings. Realistic interventions include forward contracting at favorable prices, redirecting harvests to alternative markets, adjusting storage strategies, and activating food bank partnerships before waste occurs.

\subsection{Broader Implications for Food Security}

Crop waste is not just an economic problem---it is a food security problem. The United Nations estimates that one-third of all food produced globally is lost or wasted~\citep{fao2019food}. While much of this waste occurs at the consumer level in developed countries, on-farm waste driven by economic factors represents a particularly tragic category: nutritionally valuable food that was successfully grown but never reached the market. Models that predict this waste early enough to enable intervention can directly reduce global hunger.

\cascrop{} also connects to the growing literature on climate adaptation in agriculture~\citep{lobell2011climate}. As climate change increases the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, the cascading economic effects of these events will intensify. A drought in one region will increasingly cause waste in distant regions through market mechanisms. Network-aware models like \cascrop{} are essential for anticipating these second-order effects.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations warrant discussion:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{County-level granularity.} We operate at county-crop-month resolution because this matches our training labels (RMA insurance claims). Field-level prediction would require different data sources and is left to future work.

    \item \textbf{Three commodity focus.} We evaluate on corn, soybeans, and wheat---the three largest U.S.\ field crops by area. Extension to specialty crops, fruits, and vegetables would require different market structure models.

    \item \textbf{U.S.-only.} Our dataset covers the continental United States. International extension requires different data sources and market connectivity structures, though the ECMP mechanism is domain-independent.

    \item \textbf{No causal identification.} \cascrop{} is a predictive model, not a causal one. High attention weights between counties indicate statistical association, not necessarily causal contagion. A structural causal model extension is planned for future work.

    \item \textbf{Retrospective evaluation.} We evaluate on historical data. Real-time deployment introduces additional challenges including data latency, feature freshness, and decision support integration.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Policy Implications}

Our findings have direct policy relevance:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Crop insurance pricing.} Current actuarial models for crop insurance premiums treat counties independently. Our results suggest that premium calculations should incorporate network risk---a county's exposure to economic contagion from connected regions.

    \item \textbf{USDA early warning systems.} The USDA's existing crop monitoring relies on satellite imagery and weather forecasts. Adding economic contagion modeling could substantially improve early warning accuracy, particularly for the market-driven waste events that current systems miss.

    \item \textbf{Market stabilization.} Understanding contagion pathways suggests targeted interventions: if oversupply in region A will cause waste in region B, directing surplus from A to alternative markets before the price cascade occurs could prevent waste in both regions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}

Two extensions follow naturally from this work:

\paragraph{Counterfactual intervention analysis.} Given a predicted cascade, what happens if a farmer redirects crops to an alternative market? If a policy subsidy stabilizes prices? Adding a structural causal model head to \cascrop{} would enable such ``what-if'' analysis. We deferred this to maintain focus on the contagion prediction thesis, but the ECMP attention structure provides a natural basis for intervention modeling.

\paragraph{Real-time deployment.} Deploying \cascrop{} as a live early warning system requires integrating near-real-time satellite imagery, weather data, and price feeds. The county-level aggregation and monthly time step make this computationally feasible---the bottleneck is data latency rather than model inference time.

% 9. CONCLUSION
% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Crop waste is not just a local phenomenon---it is a network phenomenon. Economic shocks propagate across regions through commodity markets, causing waste in distant counties whose crops are perfectly healthy. We introduced \cascrop{}, a graph neural network that models this contagion through two novel mechanisms: biophysical-economic disentanglement and Economic Contagion Message Passing with asymmetric shock conditioning. On a novel dataset spanning 3,000+ U.S.\ counties over 17 years, \cascrop{} significantly outperforms all baselines, with the largest improvements on the economically-driven waste events that no existing model captures. We estimate that deploying \cascrop{} nationally could enable early warning for \$1.8 billion in annual preventable crop waste.

The ECMP mechanism is domain-independent. Anywhere that asymmetric shocks propagate through a network---financial markets, supply chains, energy grids, epidemiological systems---the same architecture applies. Agriculture is the proving ground; the principle extends far beyond it.
